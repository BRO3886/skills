# Copy patterns

Headline moves taken from campaign lines by Apple, Oatly, Spotify, Porsche, Airbnb, Duolingo, Tesla, Wendy's, and Nike. Read this before drafting headlines in Stage 5.

The lines are here to show how a move is built. Never reuse one, and never write a near-copy with the product name swapped in; most are trademarks and all of them are recognisable. Attribution comes from a scraped collection that was not checked line by line, so do not quote any of these as fact in user-facing copy.

## Length

The median line in the source collection is five words. A headline that needs more than about nine is usually two ideas; give the second one to the subhead.

## Pick the move by what the post must do

| The post must | Reach for |
| --- | --- |
| Announce a release or a feature | Plain fact, self-reference |
| Explain why the product is different | Concrete contrast, redefinition |
| Show the product is in real use | Real usage as the copy |
| Make a dry technical feature memorable | Named with personality, borrowed phrase |
| Sound like a person, not a brand | Understatement, talking to the reader |

Draft three headlines per post from three different moves, then keep the one that could not belong to another product.

## The moves

### Plain fact

State what the thing does in the words a user would use. It works when the fact itself is the surprise, and it is the safest move because the claim is literally checkable against the docs.

- Pinch to zoom. — Apple
- Lots of storage for lots of photos. — Apple
- Fresh, Never Frozen — Wendy's
- It just works. — Apple

### Concrete contrast

Two short halves, with the thing the reader gives up named as plainly as the thing they get. Both halves must be concrete nouns or verbs.

- More adventure, less checklist. — Airbnb
- Blows minds, not budgets. — Apple
- Burn rubber not gasoline — Tesla
- No charger? No problem. — Apple
- Chic. Not Geek. — Apple

This is not the banned "not just X, it's Y". That form keeps X and inflates it with an abstraction ("not just a tool, a platform"). A real contrast drops X entirely and replaces it with something you can picture.

### Redefinition

Deny the category the reader has already filed the product under. Use it only when the product genuinely does not behave like its category, and the art proves it.

- Your next computer is not a computer. — Apple
- Where did the computer go? — Apple
- There are no getaway cars. — Porsche
- IT'S LIKE MILK BUT MADE FOR HUMANS — Oatly

### Self-reference

Compare the product only to itself. It reads as confident because it refuses to name a competitor, and it suits version releases.

- The biggest thing to happen to iPhone since iPhone. — Apple
- This changes everything. Again. — Apple
- Say hello to the new iPod shuffle. Again. — Apple
- The only thing that's changed is everything. — Apple

### Real usage as the copy

Put a true, oddly specific fact about how people use the product in the headline and let it carry the joke. This is the strongest move for a product with real users, and the only one that is impossible to fake: every figure and every quoted name must come from the user with permission, per the rule against invented numbers.

- Dear person who played "Sorry" 42 times on Valentine's Day, what did you do? — Spotify
- 200+ people made a playlist titled "Dump Him." — Spotify
- Thanks, 2016. It's been weird. — Spotify

### Understatement

Describe the product more modestly than the reader expects from an ad. It works for brands whose audience distrusts marketing, which includes most developers.

- Pretty Average Vanilla — Oatly
- Slightly Worse than Last Year! — Oatly
- Nobody's perfect. — Porsche
- Infrequently Asked Questions — Oatly

### Talking to the reader

Address the person holding the phone, in the moment they are in, including the fact that they are reading an ad.

- You actually read this? Total success! — Oatly
- Not sure where to go? Perfect. — Airbnb
- It looks like you've learned how to say "quitter" in Portuguese. — Duolingo
- Have a weakness for fast cars? You'd better work on it. — Porsche

### Named with personality

Give a feature a name that states its behaviour with a straight face. The name does the marketing every time it appears in the UI, so check it against the real feature name in the product before putting it on a post.

- Ludicrous Mode — Tesla
- Bioweapon Defense Mode — Tesla
- Camp Mode — Tesla

### Borrowed phrase, one word changed

Take a phrase the audience already knows and change the one word that makes it about the product. It fails outside the culture the phrase comes from, so skip it for audiences who do not read English as a first language.

- iThink, therefore iMac. — Apple
- Great powers come with great privacy. — Apple
- The plot thins — Apple
- Strangers aren't strange — Airbnb

### Coined word

Fuse the product name or its key attribute into a familiar word. One per campaign at most; it has to survive being said aloud.

- Thinnovation — Apple
- Completely Renanoed. — Apple
- Wow No Cow — Oatly

## Lines that do not earn their place

The same collection holds lines that could sit under any logo. They are the squint-test failure in text form:

- Happiness delivered.
- Your cravings, our mission.
- Fast, reliable, everywhere.
- Built for any adventure.
- Endless Possibilities

Each one is an abstract noun plus a promise. Replace it with the specific thing: the real feature, the real number, the real limit.
