# What makes copy work

This is an account of why some headlines work and others do not, argued from real campaign lines. It is meant to change how you judge a draft, so read it for the reasoning and then apply that reasoning to the product in front of you.

Nothing here is a template. The structure of a good line carries none of what made it good, because what made it good was a fact about that product and that reader. A line built by copying the shape of one of these examples will have the shape and nothing else.

The examples come from a scraped collection of campaign lines that was not checked against primary sources. Use them to follow the argument. Do not reuse them, and do not cite them as fact in anything the user will publish.

## The situation a headline is in

Nobody asked for it. It sits in a feed between things the reader chose to follow, it gets about a second, and the reader is already looking for a reason to scroll past. Every judgement below follows from that.

A line earns its second by giving the reader something in exchange for it: a fact they did not have, a small puzzle that resolves, or the sense that a person is speaking to them. A line that gives none of these is recognised as advertising before the reader reaches the end of it, and what is recognised as advertising is discounted to zero.

So the question to ask of a draft is never "does this sound good?" It is "what does the reader have after reading this that they did not have before?"

## A claim carries information only if it could be false

Apple once sold iPhone storage with “Lots of storage for lots of photos.” Nike once ran “Endless Possibilities”. The first is plainer and it is the better line, and the reason is mechanical. Negate each one. A phone with too little storage for your photos is a real thing that readers have owned and resented. Limited possibilities is not something anyone sells. A claim whose opposite nobody would make rules nothing out, and a sentence that rules nothing out tells the reader nothing.

The same test explains Wendy's “Fresh, Never Frozen”. Its negation is a real product on a real competitor's grill. The line informs the reader, and it accuses someone without naming them, in three words.

This is why specificity works, and it is worth being exact about the mechanism, because "be specific" is usually given as a rule with no reason attached. A specific claim is expensive to make if it is untrue. A reader knows this without thinking about it, and so reads specificity as evidence. Spotify's “Dear person who played ‘Sorry’ 42 times on Valentine's Day, what did you do?” contains no adjective and makes no claim about the product at all. But only a company that knows what you listen to, song by song and day by day, could have written it. The line is its own proof.

For the product you are writing about, the useful question is therefore: what do we know, or do, that a competitor could not truthfully say? The answer is usually small and concrete: a number from the docs, a limit the product chose to have, the exact name of a thing in the UI. If any competitor could run your line under its own logo without lying, the line has said nothing about this product.

## The reader should take the last step

An Apple laptop line reads “The plot thins”. The reader hears the word that should have been there, notices the swap, and arrives at "this laptop is thin". Apple never says it. The conclusion forms in the reader's head, and that is the whole value of the line: people believe conclusions they reach and discount conclusions an advertiser hands them.

Compare a line from the same collection, “Fast, reliable, everywhere”. Three conclusions are delivered finished. The reader has nothing to do, and three unsupported verdicts from an interested party are worth nothing as evidence.

The same mechanism is behind understatement. Porsche ran “Nobody's perfect.” above a set of race results it had dominated. The line says less than the facts do, and the reader supplies the difference. The praise is the reader's own, so it sticks.

The gap has a size, and it is one step. If the reader needs two inferences, or needs to know a phrase, a film, or a piece of history they may not know, they do not solve it; they leave. This is the actual reason wordplay fails with readers who use English as a second language, and the reason an in-joke fails outside the group that shares it. Decide who the reader is before deciding how much to leave unsaid.

In this pipeline the headline never appears alone, and that changes what it should say. Apple's “Your next computer is not a computer.” is unfinished as a sentence. The picture of the product finishes it. Treat the headline and the art as one statement split in two: the line opens a question and the art closes it. If your headline is complete without the art, one of the two is redundant, and the post would be stronger if the headline said less.

## Tension, and why the banned construction has none

A line that holds two things which should not fit together, such as “Small is huge.”, makes the reader look for the sense in which both are true. That looking is attention. But the resolution has to exist in the product. A contradiction that the product does not resolve is only a riddle, and readers do not forgive being made to work for nothing.

This is the reason behind the skill's ban on "not just X, it's Y". The construction imitates tension and contains none. It agrees with what the reader already thinks, that the product is an X, and then adds a larger and vaguer word on top. Nothing conflicts, so nothing needs resolving, and the larger word is an unfalsifiable claim of the kind the previous section rules out. A line like “Blows minds, not budgets.” looks similar on the surface and works differently: it names a real fear the reader has about the product, that it will be expensive, and denies it.

## A position costs something

Oatly's “IT'S LIKE MILK BUT MADE FOR HUMANS” is an argument, and one that an industry objected to. Porsche's “Honestly now, did you spend your youth dreaming about a Nissan or Mitsubishi?” insults two competitors and anyone who drives their cars. Both lines will lose some readers, and that is what makes them credible to the rest. A company willing to pay a price for saying something probably means it. A line nobody could object to has been written for nobody.

This does not mean a product should pick fights. For most software, the position is a decision the product already made: what it refuses to do, who it is not for, which tradeoff it took. Stating that plainly is a position, and it is one the docs can back.

Admitting a weakness works by the same logic. Oatly titled a sustainability report “Slightly Worse than Last Year!” A company that will print that is believed when it later prints good news. A real limit, stated without apology, is among the most persuasive things a new product can say, because it is the one thing a reader never expects an ad to contain.

## It has to sound like someone

Duolingo's reminder “It looks like you've learned how to say ‘quitter’ in Portuguese.” and Oatly's “You actually read this? Total success!” have a speaker. You can hear a tone, and you can tell what that speaker thinks of you. Compare “Your cravings, our mission.” Nobody has ever said that sentence to another person. Readers detect this register instantly, and it is the register itself, before any content, that marks a line as something to skip.

A usable check: say the draft aloud to a colleague, as if telling them about the product. If you would have to change the wording to avoid sounding strange, the wording is wrong.

Voice is also a matter of moment. The Duolingo line is written for someone who has just skipped a lesson. It knows where the reader is. A social post has a moment too: the reader is mid-scroll, slightly bored, and owes you nothing. Copy that acknowledges this tends to sound human, and copy that addresses an imagined eager customer does not.

## Sound decides what survives

The last word of a line is the one that stays, so the point belongs there. In “Blows minds, not budgets.” the point is the price, and the price is the final word. In “This changes everything. Again.” a single word in its own sentence turns a grand claim into a self-aware one, and it can only do that from the end.

Short, common words read faster and are harder to misread at a glance. Remove each word in turn and see whether the meaning survives; if it does, the word was not doing anything. The median line in the source collection is five words. That is a consequence of this kind of cutting. Treat it as a sign of where finished lines end up and never as a length to aim at.

## What does not transfer from famous brands

Much of what makes famous lines work is the fame. Apple's “Hello.” and Nike's “Just Do It” say almost nothing, and they can afford to, because the reader already knows the product and brings the meaning with them. Understatement such as “Nobody's perfect.” needs a reputation to be modest about.

A product the reader has never heard of has none of this to draw on. A cryptic line from an unknown brand is only cryptic, and modesty about an unknown product is read as an accurate description. For such a product the line has to carry the information itself: what the thing is, or what it does, in the reader's own words.

Here the early iPhone ads are the better model. “Pinch to zoom.” is a plain description of a new thing, and when a thing is new to the reader, the plain description of it is the most interesting sentence available. Wit can wait for the second line, or for the product to become known.

## Working toward a line

The order matters, because most weak copy comes from starting at the wording.

1. Write the true claim as a dull sentence, sourced from the docs. If you cannot write the dull sentence, there is no headline yet. The problem is that you do not know what to say, and no phrasing fixes that.
2. Write down what the reader currently believes or does instead. The headline lives in the difference between that and your dull sentence.
3. Find the most specific true detail that supports the claim: the number, the limit, the name.
4. Decide what the art will show, and give the headline only what is left.
5. Draft several lines that differ in what they say, not only in how they phrase it.
6. Cut each one, then move the point to the end.

## Questions to put to a draft

- Could a competitor run this unchanged without lying?
- Would anyone claim the opposite?
- What does the reader work out for themselves, and is it one step?
- Would a person say this aloud to another person?
- Does it depend on the brand already being known?
- Is the last word the point?
- Does it make sense to someone who has never seen the product?

When a draft fails one of these, rephrasing rarely rescues it. Go back to the first three steps and find something truer or more specific to say.
